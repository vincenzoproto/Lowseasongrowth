# Low Season Is Not One Problem: A Cross-Regional Evidence Framework for Hospitality Commercial Planning

**Working practitioner manuscript — venue-neutral draft**  
Author: Vincenzo Proto  
Affiliation: Low Season Growth  
Date: 24 September 2026

## Abstract

Hospitality operators often use "low season" as a single label for very different commercial problems. This practitioner paper uses four public Low Season Growth research notes to show why weak demand should instead be diagnosed through distinct evidence layers: seasonality concentration, source-market growth, hotel revenue performance, and market divergence. The evidence covers European accommodation seasonality, international versus domestic demand in the European Union, Caribbean low-season RevPAR and Latin American source-market growth, and Japan's 2026 inbound slowdown alongside record performance from selected origin markets. The cases are not directly comparable and are not presented as a ranking. Instead, they illustrate a practical decision framework for independent hotels: identify the exact weak period, determine whether the problem is demand volume or market fit, examine source-market and booking-window differences, separate pricing from demand creation, and track the commercial process that precedes occupancy. The paper also discusses the limitations of using national or regional tourism statistics for property-level decisions and proposes a transparent evidence hierarchy for hospitality practitioners.

## Keywords

hotel seasonality; low season; hospitality commercial strategy; source markets; direct bookings; hotel sales; tourism demand; RevPAR

## 1. Introduction

Low season is frequently treated as a single commercial category. In practice, a hotel can be "quiet" for very different reasons.

One property may operate in a destination where annual demand is structurally concentrated into a short peak. Another may have reasonable destination demand but weak access to the source markets that travel outside the peak. A third may face a pricing and conversion problem rather than a demand problem. A fourth may see total tourism demand decline while selected origin markets continue to grow.

Those situations should not generate the same commercial response.

The aim of this practitioner paper is not to establish a universal low-season metric. It is to show, using four source-backed evidence cases, how hospitality operators can classify weak-demand problems before selecting tactics.

The cases use public data from Eurostat, the Caribbean Hotel and Tourism Association (CHTA) and Amadeus, the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO), and the Japan Tourism Agency. Low Season Growth calculations are identified separately from the underlying official or industry-source figures.

## 2. Evidence principles

Three principles guide the framework.

### 2.1 Do not compare unlike metrics

Tourist-accommodation concentration, guest-night growth, hotel RevPAR and border arrivals describe different parts of the tourism system.

A 30-percentage-point seasonality concentration gap cannot be ranked against a RevPAR decline or an inbound-arrival change. The values may all be relevant to low-season planning, but they answer different questions.

### 2.2 Separate source data from derived calculations

The evidence base distinguishes:

1. figures published by the source institution;
2. arithmetic calculations performed by Low Season Growth;
3. commercial interpretation for hospitality operators.

Derived metrics should not be presented as official indicators from the source institution.

### 2.3 National and regional data are context, not a hotel forecast

Macro tourism evidence can help a property identify plausible demand patterns. It cannot determine the occupancy, ADR, profitability or future performance of an individual hotel.

Property decisions still require internal evidence such as booking pace, source markets, channel mix, lead time, conversion, rate positioning and account-level sales activity.

## 3. Evidence Case 1: European tourism seasonality

Low Season Growth Research Note 01 uses Eurostat tourist-accommodation data to describe the concentration of annual demand between strong and weak months.

The LSG Peak-to-Trough Concentration Gap is calculated as:

> share of annual accommodation nights in the two busiest months minus the share in the two slowest months.

Selected values include:

- Croatia: 53.1 percentage points;
- France: 27.7 percentage points;
- Italy: 26.8 percentage points;
- Lithuania: 11.3 percentage points.

The calculation is descriptive and is not an official Eurostat indicator.

### Practitioner interpretation

The evidence illustrates why "European low season" is not a useful commercial category by itself.

A market with extreme seasonal concentration requires a different planning horizon from a market with a more even annual distribution. But even national concentration can hide substantial differences between city, coastal, mountain and rural destinations.

The first diagnostic question for a hotel should therefore be:

> Which exact dates are structurally difficult for this property?

The answer should be based on property-level booking pace rather than simply on the destination's conventional high- and low-season labels.

## 4. Evidence Case 2: International versus domestic demand in Europe

Research Note 02 uses Eurostat figures for the first half of 2026.

Published figures included:

- foreign guest-night growth: +2.5%;
- domestic guest-night growth: +0.9%;
- foreign share of EU accommodation nights: 48.9%.

Low Season Growth calculated an international-to-domestic growth ratio of approximately 2.78 and estimated that roughly 73.1% of additional Q1 nights came from international visitors using Eurostat's published absolute increases.

### Practitioner interpretation

Aggregate destination demand can conceal important differences in source-market momentum.

For hotels in markets where international demand is material, weak-period planning may need to consider:

- source-market language;
- flight access;
- booking windows;
- international distribution;
- travel-trade partnerships;
- reasons to travel outside traditional peak periods.

For properties dominated by domestic demand, local corporate accounts, drive markets, repeat guests and shorter booking windows may be more relevant.

The diagnostic question becomes:

> Is the missing demand primarily a volume problem, or is the hotel failing to reach a source market that already has a reason to travel?

## 5. Evidence Case 3: Caribbean low-season hotel performance

Research Note 03 uses figures published in the 2026 Caribbean Travel Trends Report by CHTA and Amadeus.

Published figures included:

- high-season hotel RevPAR: US$183;
- low-season hotel RevPAR: US$125;
- year-end holiday RevPAR: US$283;
- Latin American demand growth: +24% year over year;
- Brazil low-season arrivals growth: +60%;
- Colombia low-season arrivals growth: +26%.

Low Season Growth calculated that the reported low-season RevPAR level was approximately 31.7% below the high-season level.

### Practitioner interpretation

The case shows two different dynamics at once.

First, there is a clear reported performance gap between low and high periods.

Second, selected source markets are already contributing to demand outside traditional peaks.

The commercial implication is that a weak period does not automatically require discounting. A hotel may need to examine whether alternative source markets, different travel purposes, partnerships or booking windows can alter the demand mix.

The diagnostic question becomes:

> Is the hotel's weak-period problem price sensitivity, or insufficient demand creation from segments able to travel then?

## 6. Evidence Case 4: Japan aggregate cooling and source-market divergence

Research Note 04 uses official JNTO and Japan Tourism Agency statistics.

JNTO reported:

- August 2026 visitor arrivals: 3,098,900;
- August year-on-year change: -9.6%;
- 14 source markets recorded their highest-ever August arrivals;
- Italy and Spain recorded their highest monthly visitor arrivals on record.

July visitor arrivals were 3,442,100.

Low Season Growth calculated that August arrivals were approximately 10.0% lower than July on a month-to-month basis.

Japan Tourism Agency accommodation statistics reported:

- July total guest nights: 54.67 million;
- July foreign guest nights: 13.58 million;
- July room occupancy: 60.8%;
- June room occupancy: 57.2%.

The accommodation survey changed its stratification basis from employee count to room count from January 2026, which requires caution in year-on-year interpretation.

### Practitioner interpretation

This case illustrates source-market divergence directly.

Total inbound demand weakened in August, yet selected markets continued to set records.

The implication for commercial planning is important:

> A falling headline market does not mean every source market is falling.

Properties should therefore avoid treating total destination demand as the only signal for whether a weak period can be influenced commercially.

## 7. A five-step weak-period diagnostic

The four evidence cases support a practical sequence.

### Step 1 — Define the weak period precisely

Do not begin with "our low season is winter."

Identify the actual problem:

- month;
- week;
- day-of-week pattern;
- gap between events;
- room category;
- booking window;
- occupancy pace.

### Step 2 — Identify whether the issue is demand volume or market fit

Ask who is travelling during those dates and whether the current proposition is relevant to them.

Weak demand from one segment may coexist with growth from another.

### Step 3 — Test the offer before changing the rate

An offer includes more than price.

Hotels should ask:

- who is this stay for?
- what problem does it solve?
- why are these dates relevant?
- what booking condition or inclusion reduces friction?

A discount cannot fix an offer that is irrelevant to the available demand.

### Step 4 — Match the route to market to the guest

The right channel depends on the target segment.

Consumer OTAs, direct web demand, corporate prospecting, group sales, travel advisors, destination partners and repeat guests solve different distribution problems.

### Step 5 — Measure the process before judging the outcome

Occupancy is an outcome metric.

Weak-period commercial work should also measure:

- qualified accounts contacted;
- replies;
- proposals;
- direct enquiries;
- conversion;
- channel mix;
- lead time;
- bookings attributable to the activity.

This matters particularly for proactive sales, where the commercial process may begin weeks or months before the stay date.

## 8. Implications for independent hotels

Independent hotels often have fewer resources than large chains, which makes prioritisation more important.

A useful low-season strategy should therefore begin with evidence triage rather than tactic generation.

The operator does not need 30 promotional ideas. The operator needs to know which class of problem is present.

The evidence hierarchy can be summarised as:

1. **property evidence** — booking pace, occupancy gaps, channel mix, rate, source markets;
2. **destination evidence** — events, air access, demand calendar and local segment mix;
3. **national/regional evidence** — seasonality and source-market context;
4. **commercial execution evidence** — outreach, proposals, response rates and conversion.

Macro tourism data is most useful when it changes the questions asked at property level.

## 9. Limitations

This paper combines evidence from different geographies and source systems.

The cases are intentionally not normalised into a single index.

Limitations include:

- different reference periods;
- different statistical units;
- regional or national aggregation;
- survey methodology changes;
- industry-source data alongside official statistical data;
- descriptive calculations that do not establish causality;
- no property-level revenue or profitability evidence.

The framework should therefore be used as a decision aid, not as a forecasting model.

## 10. Conclusion

Low season is not one problem.

The four evidence cases show that weak demand can reflect seasonal concentration, source-market composition, hotel revenue-performance gaps or divergence between aggregate and segment-level demand.

The practical lesson is sequencing.

Before selecting a tactic, a hotel should define the weak dates, identify the available demand, test market fit, choose the correct route to market and measure the commercial process.

Better diagnosis reduces the risk of treating every weak period as a pricing problem.
